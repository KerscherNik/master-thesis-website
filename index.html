Abstract

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) reconstructs a 3D scene from ordinary photographs and renders novel views of it in real time. The scene is represented as a large collection of small, semi-transparent Gaussian primitives. The method produces sharp reconstructions, but fine, high-frequency detail such as grass, foliage, and thin edges remains difficult, since adding more Gaussians recovers such detail only at the cost of a larger and slower model.

This thesis asks at what point in the training process a signal indicating missing high-frequency detail should be applied. We test the two most direct entry points: the photometric loss, which measures the rendering error, and the densification rule, which decides where Gaussians are added and removed during training. In the settings we tested, the signal is effective only when it is applied at the densification rule. Our method, Spectral-Aware Densification (SAD), compares the render and the training image in the frequency domain and measures where high-frequency detail is missing. This signal then decides where new Gaussians are placed and which existing ones are removed.

On the nine scenes of the Mip-NeRF 360 benchmark, SAD uses on average about 25% fewer Gaussians than standard 3DGS, improves SSIM and LPIPS, and renders about 60% faster, at an average PSNR at most 0.05 dB below the baseline. On two further benchmarks, Tanks-and-Temples and Deep Blending, the count reduction carries over without retuning, though at some cost in the perceptual metrics relative to the baseline. A second configuration that removes Gaussians less aggressively surpasses the baseline on all three image metrics on Mip-NeRF 360 and Tanks-and-Temples, at a Gaussian count above the baseline's. The contribution is efficiency: more reconstruction quality per Gaussian.

Method

During training, SAD compares each render against its training image in the frequency domain: both are cut into overlapping patches, transformed with a windowed FFT, and the shortfall of high-frequency energy in the render is aggregated into a per-pixel spectral deficit map. The deficit replaces the gradient heuristic on both sides of adaptive density control: Gaussians participating in high-deficit regions are cloned and split, and the usual opacity pruning is weighted so that primitives supporting deficit regions are kept while inconspicuous ones are removed. Nothing else in the training pipeline changes: SAD replaces the signal that adaptive density control reads, and everything downstream follows from that.
